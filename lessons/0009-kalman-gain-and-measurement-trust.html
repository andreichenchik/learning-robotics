Trust follows uncertainty

A prediction and a GPS reading disagree. The Kalman gain turns their uncertainties into a correction weight.

Outcome. You will compute one scalar Kalman correction, explain why its gain changes, and predict which source will pull the corrected position more strongly.

1. One robot, one GPS reading

After prediction, the robot believes it is at 10.0 m. Its predicted position standard deviation is σ⁻=0.5 m. GPS reports z=10.8 m with standard deviation σz=0.3 m.

Predictionx̂⁻ = 10.0 m
P⁻ = 0.5² = 0.25 m²
Measurementz = 10.8 m
R = 0.3² = 0.09 m²
QuestionHow far should GPS move the estimate?

P⁻ came from the prediction step in the previous lesson. R describes GPS measurement-error variance and comes from calibration, repeated tests against ground truth, or the sensor specification—not from this single reading.

2. Measure the disagreement

The innovation is what the sensor observed minus what the prediction expected:

innovation = z − x̂⁻ = 10.8 − 10.0 = 0.8 m

It says the GPS reading is 0.8 m ahead. It does not yet say whether that discrepancy is believable.

3. Turn uncertainty into trust

K = P⁻ / (P⁻ + R) = 0.25 / (0.25 + 0.09) ≈ 0.735

The scalar Kalman gain K is between zero and one. It is the fraction of the innovation applied to the prediction:

K near 0Prediction is more precise; barely react to GPS.
K = 0.5Both have equal variance; move halfway.
K near 1GPS is more precise; move close to it.
Variance means uncertainty here. A larger P⁻ makes K larger because the prediction deserves less trust. A larger R makes K smaller because the measurement deserves less trust.

4. Correct the estimate

x̂⁺ = x̂⁻ + K(z − x̂⁻)
x̂⁺ = 10.0 + 0.735 × 0.8 ≈ 10.588 m

The corrected estimate lies between the two inputs and closer to GPS because GPS has the smaller variance.

P⁺ = (1 − K)P⁻ ≈ 0.066 m²
σ⁺ = √P⁺ ≈ 0.257 m

Combining the two independent sources produces a more precise estimate than either source alone in this model. Notice that P⁺ depends on P⁻ and R, not on whether this particular innovation was 0.8 m or −0.8 m.

Primary source: Introduction to Robotics and Perception, 4.3: Sensors for Mobile Robots defines a linear measurement with additive Gaussian noise and covariance R. The recursive correction traces to Kalman's original 1960 paper.

6. The same correction in matrix form

If the state contains position and velocity but GPS observes only position, the measurement matrix selects the position entry:

x = positionvelocity H = 10
innovation: y = z − Hx̂⁻
expected innovation variance: S = HP⁻Hᵀ + R
gain: K = P⁻HᵀS⁻¹
correction: x̂⁺ = x̂⁻ + Ky
covariance: P⁺ = (I − KH)P⁻

The scalar equations were not a different algorithm. They were this matrix form with a one-value state and H=1. In the next lesson, we will execute the complete predict–measure loop.

7. Model boundary

This result assumes unbiased, independent Gaussian errors and a correct value of R. A broken GPS reading can still pull the estimate strongly if the filter claims GPS is precise. Real systems therefore validate innovations and tune noise models from data.
