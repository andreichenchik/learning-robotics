The first robot in 2D

We will move the familiar control loop onto a plane. Position is no longer one coordinate but a pose, and the wheels prevent the robot from moving sideways instantly.

Lesson outcome. You will be able to update the pose of a differential-drive robot, predict straight motion, rotation, and arcs, then send the robot to a selected point.

1. Why two coordinates are not enough

Two robots can occupy the same point (x,y) while facing different directions. Given the same "forward" command, their futures differ. The chassis's geometric state is therefore its pose:

q = (x, y, θ)

x,y locate the midpoint of the wheel axle in the world frame. Angle θ gives the direction of the local forward axis fixed to the robot.

Primary source: Introduction to Robotics and Perception: State Space for a Differential-Drive Robot. It defines pose as q=(x,y,θ) because orientation determines the available direction of motion.

2. The wheel constraint

A conventional wheel rolls forward and backward but does not slide along its axle. The robot therefore has no independent lateral-velocity command. It is controlled by two quantities:

vForward linear velocity.
ωAngular velocity.
Lateral velocityZero in the robot frame.

This constraint is called nonholonomic: the robot can reach any pose, but not through any instantaneous motion. A parking space, for example, is reachable even though a car cannot shift strictly sideways.

Primary sources: Motion Model for the Differential Drive Robot and Modern Robotics: Modeling of Nonholonomic Wheeled Mobile Robots.

3. One discrete step

The previous lesson provided the template next = current + Δt · velocity. The direction of velocity now depends on θ:

xₖ₊₁ = xₖ + Δt · vₖ cos θₖ
yₖ₊₁ = yₖ + Δt · vₖ sin θₖ
θₖ₊₁ = wrap(θₖ + Δt · ωₖ)

At θ=0, the robot faces along the world's x-axis. At θ=π/2, it faces along the world's y-axis. Function wrap keeps the angle in the convenient range from −π to π.

4. Why the drive is called differential

The left and right wheels rotate independently. Their difference produces a turn:

φ̇L = (v − Lω/2) / r
φ̇R = (v + Lω/2) / r

Here, r is wheel radius and L is the distance between the wheels. Equal speeds produce straight motion; opposite speeds produce rotation in place.

6. Point-to-point controller

The controller first finds the direction to the target, then compares it with the current θ:

α = atan2(y* − y, x* − x)
eθ = wrap(α − θ)
ω = Kθ · eθ
v = Kv · distance · max(0, cos eθ)

The cos eθ factor slows forward motion while the robot faces away from the target. This is not the only possible controller, but a minimal example that composes familiar ideas: state → error → command → new state.

Model boundary. The simulation assumes ideal rolling without slip and instant execution of wheel velocities. A real robot adds motor dynamics, traction, imprecise geometry, and odometry error.
