Uncertainty has shape

A state estimate is not merely a point. It is a claim about plausible errors—and in two dimensions, those errors can stretch and lean together.

Outcome. You will read a 2D covariance matrix, predict the orientation of its uncertainty ellipse, and distinguish estimated uncertainty from the robot's unknown actual error.

1. One estimate, many possible truths

Suppose the estimated position is μ = (0, 0). That point is the centre of our belief, not a guarantee that the robot is there. Repeating the same experiment could produce different estimation errors around it.

error e = true state x − estimated mean μ

The actual error is one unknown vector in the current run. Uncertainty describes the spread we expect across plausible errors before seeing the truth. A consistent estimator should not report a narrow belief while routinely making large errors.

2. Covariance records spread and co-variation

For a two-dimensional position, the covariance matrix is the expected outer product of the error with itself:

Σ = E[e eᵀ] = [ σx² σxy ]
[ σxy σy² ]
σx²Variance of the x error. Larger means wider horizontal spread.
σy²Variance of the y error. Larger means wider vertical spread.
σxyCovariance. Its sign says how x and y errors vary together.

Position variance and covariance have squared units: if position is measured in metres, these entries use m². Standard deviation returns to metres through σ = √variance.

3. Correlation makes the shape readable

ρ = σxy / (σx σy),   −1 ≤ ρ ≤ 1

Correlation is normalized covariance. When ρ > 0, positive x errors tend to accompany positive y errors, so the cloud leans along an upward diagonal. When ρ < 0, their signs tend to oppose each other. Zero covariance removes the lean, but it does not require equal uncertainty in both directions.

Primary source: Introduction to Robotics and Perception, 4.1: Continuous State defines multivariate Gaussian state uncertainty using a mean vector and covariance matrix, then shows how covariance determines its elliptical contours.

4. The ellipse is a contour, not a fence

For a Gaussian belief, points with equal covariance-scaled distance from the mean form an ellipse:

d² = (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ)

The ellipse d=1 follows the principal one-standard-deviation axes. In 2D it contains only about 39% of a Gaussian's probability—not 68%, which is the familiar one-dimensional result. The d=2 ellipse contains about 86%. Neither contour guarantees where the true state lies.

Why these percentages differ from 1D: d² is the sum of two squared standard-normal coordinates, so it follows a chi-square distribution with two degrees of freedom; see the NIST chi-square distribution reference.

6. What covariance cannot say

Mean and covariance fully specify a Gaussian belief, but not an arbitrary belief. One ellipse cannot faithfully represent two separate candidate locations, heavy tails, or a hard obstacle boundary. Covariance also predicts typical error; it does not reveal the unknown error of this particular run.

This limitation will matter during localization: a robot that could be in either of two similar corridors has a multimodal belief, not one elliptical cloud.
